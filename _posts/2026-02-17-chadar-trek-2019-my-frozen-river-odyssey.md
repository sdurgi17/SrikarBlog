## The Dream of the Frozen River

I first heard whispers of the Chadar Trek during my college days. It sounded mythical: walking on a frozen river in the heart of the Himalayas. I devoured articles, watched BBC documentaries, and knew instantly – I had to experience it. Having trekked in the Himalayas before, I'd often seek out fellow Chadar veterans, quizzing them on the bone-chilling cold, the sheer difficulty, and the indescribable beauty. This trek, I knew, was different. It promised colder temperatures and a unique journey without significant altitude gain, making the battle against the cold the primary challenge.

My coldest encounter before Chadar was a winter Kuari Pass trek, where temperatures dipped to around -5°C at night. Chadar, however, played in a different league entirely – nights could plummet to -35°C, and even days hovered between -10°C to -15°C. This wasn't just cold; it was a whole new game of survival.

Adding to the urgency were articles predicting its demise. Global warming, government bans – whispers that the Chadar might not last. If I wanted to do it, I couldn't postpone it for long.

## The Call to Adventure

Then, the call came. My friend Shailender rang, asking if I was in. Of course, I was! We tried to rally other friends, but none, it seemed, shared our brand of adventurous insanity. So, just the two of us, we booked the trek, flights to Leh, and began gathering the essential gear.

The day of departure arrived. My mom, ever the realist, pointed out I was about to experience a temperature drop of over 30 degrees in a single day – from Hyderabad's balmy 30°C to Leh's biting -10°C. Despite their fears, my family trusted my ability to manage. They packed me an arsenal of snacks, and my sister meticulously ensured I had every conceivable item for the trek. At the airport, with my family's well wishes and Vidya's loving reminder to "stay warm," I was a cocktail of excitement and fear. Doubts gnawed, but a fierce resolve to be cautious and protective fueled me.

## Journey to Leh: A World Transformed

Changing terminals in Delhi, the cold, smoky air was a familiar embrace, a nostalgic echo of my PayU internship days. After a quick nap on an inclined chair, I boarded the flight to Leh. This flight itself was an adventure – soaring over snow-capped Himalayas. I’d specifically booked a window seat, and after an initial blanket of clouds, we were treated to a spectacular panorama. The pilot, a gracious guide, even pointed out a couple of pristine lakes below.

Upon landing, a series of stern precautions followed: drink water, stay warm, rest for two days to acclimatize, and visit the health center if AMS symptoms appeared. Stepping out into the -10°C air, the cold immediately clamped onto my legs, quickly numbing my nose and cheeks. My jacket, however, bravely shielded my upper body. I met Shailender and our fellow trekkers, and we made our way to the hotel.

## Acclimatization in Leh: The Waiting Game

"Julley!" (Greetings in Ladakhi) welcomed us to the hotel. A new rule for Chadar 2019 meant a mandatory three-day stay in Leh for acclimatization, followed by a medical checkup. Only with medical approval could we embark on the trek.

Our first day was spent mostly horizontal, our bodies protesting any prolonged exposure to the brutal cold. Even phone calls were a chilly ordeal, requiring bare hands. Dinner brought a briefing and introductions – a chance to share our trekking histories. With multi-day Himalayan treks and a mountaineering course under my belt, I was, by far, the most seasoned.

The hotel offered no running water; buckets delivered water twice daily, with strict warnings against spills that would instantly freeze. Sleep was a strange, superimposed state of dreaming and thinking, awake and asleep, a prelude to the surreal journey ahead.

Waking to a world blanketed in fresh snow and frost-covered windows, we ventured out. Children played in the snow, cheerfully accepting chocolates and asking for more tomorrow. Stok Kangri, a trekkable peak above 20,000 feet, loomed majestically, its crescent top inviting dreams of future ascents.

## Final Preparations and Medical Clearance

After breakfast, a local tour took us to a monastery, Leh Palace, and the bustling market, offering breathtaking views. We even visited the famous "3 Idiots" school with its "Rancho" cafe. Leh's unique landscape, vast plains punctuated by sparse buildings and encircled by the grand Himalayas, left a lasting impression. As evening approached and temperatures plummeted, we returned to the hotel.

A returning batch of Chadar trekkers, beaming with success, shared tales of their excellent guides and porters. We congratulated them, and they, in turn, wished us luck.

Next, the medical checkup – a significant wait, then basic questions about family history and exercise. I reported no breathing difficulties or nausea; I was fully acclimatized. The doctor confirmed my fitness with oximeter and BP readings. Hurray! All our team members received the green light.

A final trip to Leh market, now bustling with franchise stores and shawl shops, saw me buying woolen socks and a flask. Pashmina shawls for the family completed the shopping spree.

## The Chadar Awaits!

That night, we embraced our tents. The sleeping bags, surprisingly warm with their two layers, demanded a wrestling match to zip up but promised welcome insulation. A van arrived – it was time! We were finally leaving for Chadar, embarking on a five-day journey through the majestic valleys of Zanskar.

Our path led towards Chilling, 70 km from Leh, a drive so picturesque it had us all exclaiming about the beauty of Kashmir. Military camps dotted the landscape, and we stopped for a comical photo session. Our next stop was a gurudwara, a beacon in the snow-covered plains, mostly cared for by military personnel. They welcomed us warmly, offering hot tea, bread pakoda, and biscuits – a true act of kindness in the harsh environment.

Resuming our journey, we traced the banks of the frozen Indus river, awestruck by the spectacle outside our windows. Finally, Chilling. The river was a solid sheet of ice, inviting a moment of playful skating. We met our trek leader, a local from Zanskar, a town itself an 80 km trek away across the very frozen river we were about to walk. The adventure was truly about to begin.
